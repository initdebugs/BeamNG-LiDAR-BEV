The Vision-Only Ladder

How far can the car go on cameras alone — no depth buffer, no annotation channel, no laser anywhere? Much further than the last report implied. There are four honest rungs between “LiDAR in disguise” and “true Tesla”, and three of them fit your machine.

The idea

“Vision only” is a ladder, not a switch

The last report treated cameras as one decision. It isn’t — it’s a ladder of increasingly honest vision, and each rung is a separate, testable project. At the bottom, the camera secretly reads the simulator’s depth buffer (which is what your LiDAR always did). At the top, a neural network looks at pixels and imagines the world, which is what Tesla actually ships. The new research maps every rung in between, with measured speeds for each.

The one-line summary of days of research: a car that genuinely drives on cameras alone is achievable on your PC — and the best route there uses no neural network at all for the safety-critical part.

The single biggest finding: stereo vision — two cameras acting like two eyes — is nearly free, needs zero AI, and meets your braking-accuracy requirement on paper. Real Tesla can’t use it (they refused to mount camera pairs). You can, because in a simulator a perfectly calibrated pair with any spacing you like costs nothing.

The ladder

Five rungs, bottom to top

0

Engine depth — the previous report’s plan

Cameras in Tesla’s geometry, but range and labels come straight from the simulator.

Fits easilyno AIalready measured live

This is the baseline from last time: eight cameras whose depth and semantic labels are read from the engine. It looks like vision and drives on perfect data. Nothing new here — except a new role. Every higher rung can be switched on one piece at a time and compared against this rung’s perfect answer, frame by frame. That turns rung 0 from “the cheat” into the measuring instrument — a luxury no real-world team has.

1

Stereo — real vision, zero neural networks

Pair the cameras up. Depth comes from geometry, the way your own eyes do it.

Fits easily~2 ms per pair on GPUmeets AEB spec on paperproven drivable (Daimler, 2013)

Two cameras a known distance apart see every object shifted slightly between them; the shift gives the distance. The classic GPU algorithm for this (semi-global matching) costs about 1.5–2 milliseconds per camera pair on a card weaker than yours — so your whole eight-camera rig, rebuilt as four stereo pairs, computes depth for roughly 13% of one GPU. The output is a metric 3D point cloud: exactly the data structure your entire existing pipeline already eats.

Accuracy is set by how far apart the pair sits. Real cars are stuck with ~30 cm behind a windshield; you can mount a pair 0.5–1 m apart with perfect calibration and zero lens distortion. At 1280×960 with a forward-looking pair, the math gives under 1 m of range error at 50 m — which is the emergency-braking requirement, met without any AI. This isn’t speculative territory either: Daimler drove 100 km of public German roads in 2013 on essentially this method.

What you gain

  • Genuine vision-only depth — nothing read from the engine
  • Dense measurement (every pixel), where LiDAR gave you rings with gaps
  • Hills handled by methods (“stixels”, road-profile fitting) that map directly onto your existing cell-referenced ground logic

What it costs

  • Depth gets noisy with distance — ~10× noisier per point than LiDAR, so your existing per-cell accumulation becomes mandatory, not optional
  • The kerb-at-25 m requirement sits at the edge of what stereo has ever published (~20 m is typical) — reachable on paper, unproven in practice
  • A new phantom-obstacle type: depth “smears” at object edges, which your LiDAR-tuned filters were never designed for (a known, cheap fix exists)
2

Small AI, trained on BeamNG’s own free labels

Replace the annotation channel: a network learns to see road, cars, lanes, kerbs — from pixels.

Fits~6–9 ms for all 8 camerasdata capture ≈ minutestraining ≈ hours

Here the semantic understanding becomes learned instead of given. The trick that makes this cheap: BeamNG will render a perfectly labelled version of every camera frame for free. Drive around for five minutes recording eight cameras, and you have a dataset the size of ones research papers are built on — with pixel-perfect labels, for your exact rig, your exact renderer. There is no “will it transfer to the real world” problem, because the sim is the world.

The speeds are comfortable: a modern small segmentation network plus a nano object detector plus a lane detector, batched across all eight cameras, lands around 6–9 ms total — inside the budget the simulator leaves you. And in-domain results from comparable synthetic datasets suggest ~84% accuracy from a vanilla network, before any tuning. Combined with rung 1 you get the honest division of labour: geometry from stereo, meaning from AI — which is how most real (non-Tesla) autonomy stacks actually work.

One bonus that matters to this project: a segmentation network reads lane paint from the colour image, so markings work on every map — including the ones where the annotation channel calls the paint “asphalt”.

3

A real Tesla-style network — smaller than we thought

One network watches all eight cameras and directly outputs the bird’s-eye world.

Fits, with care2.5–8 ms measured elsewhererevises the last report

This is the finding that changes the previous report’s verdict. Last time, “occupancy networks” were declared impossible — correctly, for the famous ones (340–555 ms each). But the research turned up their fast siblings, and they are startlingly quick: FlashOcc runs a full six-camera occupancy pipeline in 2.5–5 ms on an RTX 3090 — a card comparable to yours. BEVDet with TensorRT: ~7 ms. These are measured numbers from deployed code, not paper claims.

The catch is not speed — it’s everything around speed. You would need a training pipeline for 3D labels (your accumulated LiDAR voxel store could generate them, a neat full-circle), a TensorRT deployment on Windows, and headroom for the fact that all published numbers came from GPUs doing nothing else, while yours also renders the game. Evidence says the data side is fine — comparable models train on a few thousand frames when train and test share a world — but this rung is a real machine-learning project, not an integration.

If it works, this is the genuine article: pixels in, drivable-space out, one network — the same architecture family Tesla describes, at hobby scale.

4

End-to-end — pixels in, steering out

The network doesn’t just see; it drives.

Surprisingly cheap to runExpensive to trustwild-card idea

The famous end-to-end systems (UniAD and friends) stay impossible — half a second per frame on datacenter hardware. But the research found a twist: the winning agent on the CARLA simulator leaderboard, TCP, is a single camera and a small network that would run in well under 10 ms on your card. End-to-end driving isn’t compute-limited at all. It’s limited by training data and by brittleness — and by the fact that it outputs steering, not an inspectable world, so your planner, AEB, overlays and BEV all become bystanders.

The brainstorm-grade idea hiding here: your current LiDAR stack could be the driving teacher. Let it drive for hours, record camera frames alongside its steering and pedals, and train a small network to imitate it — the exact “privileged teacher” recipe the CARLA winners use. Not the recommended path, because one un-debuggable network replaces your entire legible pipeline. But as a side experiment — “can a network learn to drive by watching my planner?” — it is genuinely within reach, and almost nobody can say that.

Dead ends

What stays impossible — and why it’s fine

Knowing what not to attempt is half the value of the research. Each of these was checked, and each has a cheaper substitute on the ladder.

IdeaVerdictIn simple terms
One camera guessing distance (mono-depth AI) for braking Dead The best models are off by 2–7 m at braking ranges; your AEB needs ~1 m. Worse, the error is a per-scene bias, not random noise — it fools the exact same way every frame. Stereo replaces it outright.
Depth from a single moving camera (motion parallax) Dead ahead The geometry is blind precisely in the direction of travel — the one place AEB threats live — and a car moving with you produces no parallax at all, so it reconstructs at infinity. Fine as side-view bonus evidence; never the main ranging channel.
Time-to-contact from optical flow Not for AEB Tells you when you’d hit, never how far — and your entire brake trigger, standoff and phantom filtering are built on distance. Cheap enough (6 ms) to keep as a second-opinion channel, nothing more.
Flat-ground projection (the classic lane-detection shortcut) Dead on hills Assumes the world is a billiard table. On a 3% grade it places the road at 30 m out at 75 m — a 45 m error. Your project made hills a first-class requirement; this fails them structurally.
The big-name networks (SurroundOcc, UniAD, full occupancy transformers) Still dead 40–555 ms per frame on hardware better than yours. Unchanged verdict — but their fast siblings on rung 3 do fit.
Tesla’s literal 36 Hz Capped The sensor transport tops out at 16–18 Hz regardless of what consumes the frames. Everything above assumes ~16 Hz; your LiDAR loop runs 25 Hz today, so the control loop retuning from the last report still applies.
Unfair advantages

Three things you have that Tesla doesn’t

  • Perfect stereo, any spacing. Real cars fight calibration drift, windshield distortion and packaging; you type a number into a mount definition. This one advantage is what makes braking-grade vision possible without AI.
  • Free perfect labels, forever. Every frame can be born with pixel-perfect depth and semantics. Data volume is a solved problem before you start — five minutes of driving equals a published research dataset. The variable that matters is variety (maps, weather, times of day), not quantity.
  • A built-in oracle. At any moment you can render what the AI should have answered and diff it, per pixel, per frame, live. Every rung can be validated against the rung below before you trust it. Real-world teams pay millions for pale imitations of this loop.

One more, worth naming: as far as the research could find, nobody has published a project that trains perception networks on BeamNG’s own ground truth and drives on them in the loop. The recipe is proven in CARLA and GTA V; for this engine, you’d be first.

Honest risks

What could still sink it

  • The shared GPU is the unmeasured number. Every speed above was measured on a GPU doing nothing else. Yours renders BeamNG, eight camera views, and runs the vision stack, and Windows slices the card between them rather than sharing nicely. All the arithmetic says it fits with ~2× headroom; only a live measurement says it truly does.
  • The kerb question from the last report is still the kerb question. Stereo improves the odds (denser than LiDAR, and the pair geometry is yours to choose), and the math says a 10 cm kerb at 25 m is ~6 standard deviations above the noise with the right pair — but published stereo work rarely goes past 20 m. This remains the first thing to measure, and it now has a concrete test: one wide-baseline pair, one kerb, one afternoon.
  • New phantom-braking homework. Stereo’s edge-smearing produces coherent streaks of false points behind objects — a failure type your despeckle/support/porosity filters never met, and one that survives them because it isn’t speckle. The standard fix (discard points near depth discontinuities) is cheap, but the AEB checklist re-run is not optional.
  • Everything from the last report still applies. The beamngpy upgrade, the licence clock, the 3–4 month re-derivation of planner and AEB constants against a new sampling pattern — the ladder sits on top of that work, it doesn’t replace it.
Recommendation

Climb in order, validate at every rung

The ladder isn’t just a taxonomy — it’s the build order. Each rung swaps one component and keeps the rung below as its regression test:

  1. Build rung 0 first (the last report’s plan, unchanged). It gets the whole stack driving on cameras and becomes the oracle for everything after.
  2. Run the kerb measurement before anything else — one stereo pair against one kerb at 20–30 m. It’s the cheapest experiment with the most veto power over the whole design.
  3. Swap engine depth for stereo depth (rung 1), pair by pair, diffing against the oracle. Add the edge-culling filter; re-run the phantom-braking checklist. At the end of this step the car is genuinely driving on vision — a complete, publishable result with zero neural networks.
  4. Swap engine labels for a trained segmentation network (rung 2). Capture, train overnight, A/B against the annotation channel live. Lane paint on every map comes with it.
  5. Then decide whether rung 3 (the true Tesla architecture) or the rung 4 wild card (teach a network to imitate your planner) is the more interesting summit — by then you’ll have the data pipeline, the deployment tooling, and the measurements to choose with numbers instead of guesses.

Realistic effort on top of the base camera port: rung 1 ≈ +3–6 weeks, rung 2 ≈ +4–8 weeks, rung 3 ≈ +2–3 months. But rungs 1–2 already deliver the headline: a car driving BeamNG on cameras alone, with braking accuracy that comes from geometry you can prove rather than a network you have to trust.
